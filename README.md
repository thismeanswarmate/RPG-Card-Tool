# Storyloom

Eleven tools on one page, switched from the top menu.

**Perils** builds a dangerous place by rolling on tables, in steps: a premise (theme, what stood there before, and the kind of place and danger), then tables for the place itself and for what makes it dangerous, with a personality for whoever is behind it when the danger can think. Roll everything at once, reroll single tables, pick results by hand and lock the ones to keep. The result is written up as a brief. Ten kinds of place and six kinds of danger, each with tables of its own.

**Settlements** builds a trading post, village, town, city, capital or fortress town the same way, in five steps: foundations, people, rule and order, places, and trouble and talk. Each kind of settlement has tables of its own for what it is known for, how many live there and one thing only that kind has. The result is written up as a brief and given a name.

**Setting** is the front door. Answer five questions — size, genre, landscape, focus and depth — and it generates one connected setting from the Worlds, Lore and Stories decks: a map of named places, the factions and other powers at work, the people who live there, and stories waiting to happen. It is built in layers so each part points at the others: a faction's sacred place is a real spot on the map and its founder is one of the people. A built-in name generator names everything in a few related "tongues"; any name can be redrawn or typed over, and the change shows wherever the thing is mentioned. The setting is drawn as an ink map on parchment (a blueprint for science fiction): each place is a region of land with its terrain's wash, and sites are marked. Under the map is a legend: every layer (land names, kinds of land, colours, borders, emblems, pictures, shallows, lakes, rivers, water and sea names, each rank of settlement, sites, dangerous places, their names, the names of bordering worlds, and the title, compass and scale) can be turned on and off, and the choice is remembered in the browser. The saved SVG is the map as shown. The map is made from the setting and its seed, so it always matches the text, and can be saved as an SVG. Settings are saved separately from the other tools, repeat exactly for the same seed, and export as Markdown.

A new setting starts one of two ways. **Let me build it for you** generates the whole thing from five answers, now including settlements and dangerous places rolled on the tables of those tools. **I’ll build it** starts empty and is built by hand: add a place, fill it with sites, factions, people, settlements, dangers and other things, then add the next place. Each addition is described in terms of what is already there. Either kind of setting can be edited afterwards: add, rewrite, rename, reroll or remove any part.

**Lore** grows a web of linked story elements — factions, figures, events, places, objects, materials and creatures. Each element takes a primary cue and up to four details; a detail can hold links, completed with a new cue or an element already in the web, and any link can be expanded into an element of its own. Optional Deities, Emblems and Names packs, a one-click web generator, and a written account of the whole web.

**Stories** builds story prompts from Character, Drive, Subject, Obstacle and Trait cards, in five patterns from a single story seed to two characters on a collision course. Turn a card to its next cue, lock what you like and redraw the rest. Optional Fantasy, Science fiction and Horror packs, and a Dynamics pack that adds a bond between two characters and a question to answer. Three more optional packs: **Mystery** (a genre pack), **Curios** (remarkable objects and words to describe them) and **Pasts**, which adds what once happened between the lead and someone or something else, and why it matters again now.

**Characters** builds a cast: each character draws one card for Role, Background, Motivation, Strength, Flaw, Turning point, Secret and Catalyst from a core deck plus Fantasy, Science fiction, Modern and Horror packs. Lock the cards you like, redraw the rest, add your own cards, and read the result as a written portrait.

**Worlds** is a browser tool for building story settings from cards. Draw a Terrain, tuck Sites, Epithets, Legacies, Traits and
Omens under its edges, and arrange the resulting microsettings into a world map.

- **Two tabs:** *Custom* is the hands-on table. *Auto* deals a whole world in one click: a meta row, then 5–20 microsettings (by map size) alternating simple and complex, which you can then edit like any other. Each tab keeps its own world.
- **Guided or free:** follow the six-step guide (with an optional "complex" mode that draws two and lets you keep one or both), or draw from any pile.
- **Table:** drag cards, tuck them under a Terrain or Site edge, double-click to turn a card to its next cue, pan and zoom.
- **World tools:** a sideboard of face-up cards, a world-wide meta row, discards, links between cards, and notes per microsetting.
- **Written descriptions:** every cue carries a sentence, and the cards of a microsetting are woven into prose that names the places and sites around them. Select a card to read its area, or open the chronicle for the whole world.
- **Expansions:** nine optional packs, all off until you switch them on in Decks. Three genre packs (fantasy, science fiction, horror) and three biome packs (frost, coast, desert) add more of the six basic card types. Three creative packs add new kinds of card: Quests, Questions about a place's people, and Lore with Twists. Tick Advanced to choose exactly which card types each pack contributes.
- **Decks:** an original starter deck plus your own cards, added in the deck manager.
- **Saving:** the world is kept in the browser and can be exported to or imported from a JSON file.

## Pictures

The Setting tool can make a picture of every place, power, thing and person, using [ComfyUI](https://www.comfy.org/) running on your own
computer with the Qwen Image 2.1 model. Nothing is sent anywhere else. It makes one picture at a time, sends nothing while ComfyUI has
other work in its queue, never touches jobs it did not submit, and asks ComfyUI to free its memory when a batch ends. Pictures are kept
in the browser.

- **Running the app on the same computer** (`npm run dev` or `npm run preview`): it works as is. Requests go through `/comfy`, which the
  dev server passes on to `http://127.0.0.1:8188`.
- **Opening the published site**: the page talks to `http://127.0.0.1:8188` directly, and ComfyUI turns away pages it has not been
  told to allow. In ComfyUI Desktop, open Settings → Server-Config, put the site's address (for example
  `https://thismeanswarmate.github.io`, with no path) in "Enable CORS header", and restart ComfyUI. The Pictures panel shows the exact
  address to enter. The browser may also ask for permission to reach the local network. A page opened as a file is always blocked.
- **From another device, such as a phone**: give ComfyUI a secure (`https://`) address that only your own devices can reach, for
  example through a private network tool, and enter that address in the Pictures panel. The "Enable CORS header" setting is still needed.

## Development

```
npm install
npm run dev      # local dev server
npm test         # unit tests
npm run build    # static site in dist/
```

**Decks.** Setting, Lore and Stories each have a Decks button, like the one in Worlds, for choosing what is in the mix. In Stories it lists the core deck and every pack, and with Advanced each kind of card within one. In Lore it lists the element types and the extras. In a setting it lists the decks for lands and sites, the decks for people and stories, and whether there are deities; the choice applies to whatever is added or redrawn from then on.

**Races**, **Cultures** and **History** are three more generators on one shared engine. Each aspect is drawn like a card and shown as the few options that came up: pick one, redraw for a fresh set, or lock it. A race has a build, a mark, its looks (skin, hair, eyes and features), an origin, a home, a gift and its burden, a lifespan and a reputation. A culture has what it prizes, who governs, its households, belief, a custom, a taboo, what it is known for, its dress and what divides it. A history is told in ages, each with a name, a beginning, who held sway, what was done and at what cost, an ending and what remains; ages can be added and removed. Each has a core deck and optional fantasy, science-fiction and horror decks, chosen under Decks.

**Pictures everywhere.** Every element can have a picture: in a setting, the sites, settlements and dangerous places as well as the places, powers, things and people; and in each standalone tool, whatever is on screen (a world's area, a character, a lore element in the Read view, a story, a peril, a settlement, a race, a culture, a history). Standalone pictures are prompted from the written description and use the ComfyUI address set under Pictures in a setting.

**Races, cultures and history in a setting.** A generated setting now comes with the races of its world, a culture for some of its places and its history in ages, drawn on the same cards as the standalone tools and from the setting's own genre decks. A culture belongs to a place and says which race mostly keeps it. In a hand-built setting, add a race or the history to the world and a culture to any place. Each can be edited aspect by aspect, renamed, pictured, minimised and removed like everything else, and all three are in the text export.

**Ties to race, culture and history.** Every person in a setting has a race and a culture: the culture of the place they live in where there is one, and usually the race that keeps it. A faction is founded in an age of the history and among a culture; events, objects and deities date from an age; a settlement keeps the culture of its place. These are written into the descriptions, listed under “Tied to” in both directions, can be changed while editing, and a portrait is told how the person's race looks.

**More kinds of race.** The optional Fantasy, Science fiction and Horror decks for Races now cover every aspect, drawing on familiar kinds (the tall and ageless, the tusked and green, the small and grey, the pale and hungry) alongside new ones. An optional Animal deck adds beast-folk traits. Any of them can be used alone by unticking Core under Decks, and a setting can switch the Animal deck on for its own races.

**Theme marks.** Every deck has a small mark of its own: a diamond for the core and starter decks, a star for fantasy, a ringed planet for science fiction, a skull for horror, a paw for animal, a magnifying glass for mystery, a snowflake, wave and sun for the frost, coast and desert lands, and so on. The mark is shown on every card and beside every draw, in all the tools, and next to each deck's name in the Decks dialogs, which serve as the key.

**History in every theme.** The optional Fantasy, Science fiction and Horror decks for History now cover every part of an age, from its name to what remains of it, and an Animal deck tells the past of beast-folk. Each can tell a whole history alone with Core unticked. In a setting, the history follows the setting's genres, and the Animal tick box in its Decks dialog applies to the history as well as the races.

**Emblems.** An emblem is put together from a shape, a field pattern, a border, three colours and an icon: ten shapes, twenty-five patterns, eight borders, eighteen colours and over fifty icons, in three arrangements. The Emblems tool shows a sheet of ideas, lets every part be chosen, and saves the result as an SVG. In a setting, every land, race and culture bears an emblem, made from the setting itself and suited to the country where it can be (a coast tends to an anchor or a ship). People, powers, things, settlements and cultures wear the emblems of what they belong to as badges, which lead there when clicked. While editing, an emblem can be redrawn or opened and changed part by part. On the map, each place carries its emblem to the left of its name, with the smaller emblems of the cultures kept there above it.

**Rarer settlements.** In a setting, the kind of a settlement is rolled by rarity: villages are commonest, then towns, trading posts and fortress towns, with cities few and at most one capital in a world. The map draws each kind with its own symbol, growing with its rank from a small circle for a village to a starred ring and capital letters for the capital.

**Water on the map.** The sea is one plain colour to the edge of the sheet, with paler shallows round the land. Places that lie side by side now always meet, so no pocket of sea is left between them. Lakes sit inside places, more often in wet country than dry. Each kind of land has its own least and greatest lake and its own lean toward small ones: most lakes are ponds, a few are broad waters, forest and taiga hold the broadest, a rift valley the longest, grassland and desert only small ones, and marsh and moor mostly pools with now and then a mere. A broad lake is drawn as large as the place has room for, and rivers run from a spring or a lake to that place's own coast, keeping clear of names and marks. Lakes and rivers are drawn as the sea is, with the same dark shore and pale shallows; a river swells and narrows along its length, widens toward its mouth, and runs into its lake or the sea with no line across the join. The land carries no decorative terrain symbols.

**Bordering worlds and seas.** A setting may be joined by land to a neighbouring world on each of its four sides, one at most per side; a generated setting has one to three. On the map a neighbour is plain land along the whole of its edge of the sheet, corners included, which swells inward to meet this world along a dot-and-dash frontier, with its name on it. Where two neighbours lie on adjoining sides they meet in the corner, so no corner is left to the sea. Every side without a neighbour faces a named sea. Lakes and rivers are named as well, a river's name set along its course. Neighbours are still only a name and a side; while editing they can be added, renamed, moved and removed. The names of seas, lakes and rivers follow from the setting and cannot yet be changed.

**Names of lands.** A land's name now takes one of six forms: a single word (Marrowick), a feature of somewhere (the Vale of Marrow), a name and a feature (Marrow Reach), a describing phrase (the Drowned Fields), someone's place (Maren's Rest) or the land of something (the Land of Rooks). The feature word suits the country, so a forest may be a Weald and a glacier the Ice. Things within a land borrow the short part of its name. While editing, a land's form can be chosen from a list, and drawing its name again keeps to that form.

**Uneven water.** Each open side of the map has a sea of its own width, from a narrow strip to a broad ocean, and neighbouring worlds differ in size and outline, so the seas between them differ too. Lakes range from ponds to broad waters with bays. Rivers can run far, crossing several places on the way to the sea, and still keep clear of every name and mark.

**A map built land-first.** The map is now made the way land is: the ground comes first and places are drawn on it afterwards. Each place raises the ground round its centre; where that ground, roughened at three scales, falls below the sea is the coast, so the shore has gulfs, bays, headlands and offshore islands. Places are the parts of the land nearest each centre, and a neighbouring world is simply land that carries on off its edge of the sheet. Water shut in by land is closed up, so no pocket of sea is left between places.

**Kinds of land.** Every kind of land has rules of its own, read from its name, in `src/terrain.ts`:
- *How common it is.* Forest, steppe, heath, moor, highlands and savanna turn up often; calderas, atolls, archipelagos, glaciers, salt flats and the stranger lands are rarities.
- *Where it lies.* North is cold and south is hot: glaciers, tundra and fjords are placed to the north, dunes, deserts and savanna to the south. Islands, atolls and coastal lands go to the rim of the world, and steppe, plateaus and calderas to its heart.
- *How it is drawn.* Island lands are groups of islands standing off from the mainland in open water: an archipelago or floating isles a scatter of fair-sized islands, barrier islands a chain of long thin ones, shoals and kelp forests many small islets. An atoll is a thin ring open to the sea on one side. A lagoon is coast with a sheltered water behind a spit of land, joined to the sea by a narrow mouth; a sea of methane is one great lake, and a wadi has its riverbed. Lands that need the sea (islands, atolls, lagoons and coasts) are placed toward open ocean and face it, and bordering worlds keep off the sides they lie on. Moors are riddled with pools, and marsh, fen and mangrove are full of them and run with several streams; tundra has a few thaw ponds, karst small sinkhole pools, a caldera one round crater lake, a rift valley long narrow lakes, an oasis a single pool. Floodplains, estuaries, deltas and canyons always have their river; desert, ice and frozen sea hold no lakes. Fjords have narrow arms of the sea reaching inland, and with karst and sea cliffs the most deeply cut coasts; dunes and steppe have smooth ones.

**Words that can be made.** In a setting's descriptions, a word that names something the setting could hold is coloured, with a + beside it while editing. Only words that make sense are offered: one thing spoken of as a thing ("a council", "the old mill"), not things in general (towns, floods), doings (bells that will not ring), likenesses (skin the colour of copper), set phrases (salt fish), kinds of land (the ash waste) or anything in quotation marks. Any person spoken of can be made as what they are said to be, so "a deposed ruler" becomes a deposed ruler. Once made, the thing's own name takes the place of the words that mentioned it, as a link to it, and the + is gone; if the thing is removed, the words come back.

**Map view.** A setting opens in the map view: the map on one side with its tools, and beside it a panel showing whatever is picked. Click a land, a site, a settlement, a dangerous place or an emblem and the panel turns to it, with the land outlined on the map; click the sea, or World, for the overview, bordering worlds, history, races and stories. While editing, + Land above the map adds a land, and the buttons under the map add a site, faction, person, settlement, dangerous place, culture or other thing to the land that is picked (or a race, history or bordering world to the world). Whatever is added appears on the map and in the panel. The legend is folded under the map. The Map view / Page view button in the header switches to the single long page and back, and the choice is remembered.

**Lands and features.** Terrain comes in two sizes. A *land* is a whole country (forest, steppe, highlands, marsh, desert, tundra and so on, some sixty kinds including Rainforest, Prairie, Downs, Farmland, Mountain Range, Hill Country, Lake Country, River Valley and Fenland). A *feature* is one formation within a land: a fjord, headland, lagoon, delta, atoll, reef, barrier islands, caldera, volcano, gorge, great lake, hot springs, grove, hidden valley and the like, about forty kinds. Every land is made up of two to four features that suit its climate, its country and whether it has a coast; each has a name and a line of description, and can be added, changed or removed while editing. Features are drawn on the map: islands and atolls offshore, a lagoon or sea inlets cut into the coast, lakes of their own size, and named spots for the rest. Kinds that are only one formation are no longer dealt as lands (older settings keep the lands they have), and places in space (asteroid field, orbital ring, dome colony, derelict fleet, methane sea, terraformed plain) are set aside in a category of their own and not used for now.

**World map and close-up.** The world map shows the lands with their names and emblems, the water, and only the great places: the capital and the cities. Click a land and the map draws in on it, without moving the page, showing its features, every site, settlement and dangerous place, and the people, factions and things that belong there. Click any of those, or the land's own name or emblem, and its description opens in a box over the map; Close, Escape or a click outside puts it away. Click the sea, World, or ← World map to draw back out. The legend can switch features, people, and factions and things on and off like any other layer.

**Features hold everything.** A land holds only its features, and the features hold the rest: every site, settlement, dangerous place, person, faction, thing and culture of a land lies in one of its features. A land's entry lists its features, each with what lies in it. While editing, a land offers only + Feature; each feature offers everything else, and what is added there stays there. A new land arrives with one feature and its first site in it; removing a feature moves what it held to another of the land's. On the map, a land seen closely is divided between its features by fine dotted lines, like lands inside the land, and every mark stands on the ground of the feature it lies in. Settings made before this are put in order the first time they are opened; a land that has no features yet keeps showing what it held until one is added.

**Which features go where.** Each feature belongs to certain sorts of country (woodland, open country, high country, wetland, dry country, cold country, strange country) and turns up only in a land of one of them, as well as suiting its climate and needing a coast where it is of the coast. So dunes have buttes, oases and wadis but no glaciers or groves; an ice sheet has glaciers and ice caves; a mountain range has passes, calderas and gorges; farmland has none of those. Every kind of land has at least three features it can hold even without a coast.

**Features as small lands.** On the map each feature is drawn as a small land within its land: its own ground, washed in a colour of its kind over the land's colour, with a fine border, and (when the land is looked at closely) its name lettered at the middle of its ground with its kind beneath, as a land's is. Clicking a feature's ground on the world map looks closely at its land; clicking it once there opens the feature. The borders between features wander as the borders of lands do, bent by the same roughness that shapes the coast, and features differ in size, so that each reads as a land mass of its own.

**Features that look like what they are.** A feature's ground takes the shape of the thing itself. A lone island or tidal island is an island standing clear of the shore; an atoll is a ring round still water; barrier islands, shoals, reefs, kelp forests and sea stacks are groups of islands offshore; a peninsula is a long arm of land and a headland a short blunt one; a sand spit is a thin curving finger; a delta is a fan of low land cut by the river's mouths; a bay is a broad curve of sea let into the land and an estuary a funnel; a fjord has arms of the sea; a caldera, great lake, sinkhole, oasis, hot springs and tar pits have their water. Islands and land thrust out into the sea are the whole of their feature, lettered on themselves, and what lies in such a feature stands on it. Each kind of feature also has a colour of its own: ice pale, a grove green, a volcano dark, sand for a wadi or butte.
